Overview

Vital signs, without touching the patient

Smart watches, chest belts and ECG electrodes all need skin contact, which is uncomfortable for continuous monitoring. Millimeter-wave (mmWave) radar offers a contactless alternative: it senses the tiny movements of the chest through clothing, and heart rate and breathing rate can be recovered from the phase of the reflected signal.

The catch is noise. Motion artifacts, multipath reflections and other people in the field of view distort the raw signal. We build and benchmark three denoising frameworks on the same radar dataset: a classical BPF + FFT baseline, a DWT-Kalman-AR signal-processing pipeline, and a CNN + BiLSTM + Attention deep learning model. The deep model performs best on every metric, reaching an MAE of 3.12 bpm for heart rate and 2.99 bpm for breathing rate. Both are under the <4 bpm reliability level commonly reported for radar-based monitoring.

Contactless

No electrodes, straps or watches. The radar measures chest displacement from up to 1.6 m away, so monitoring can run continuously without bothering the subject.

Noisy by nature

Body motion, multipath echoes and range-bin misalignment bury a heartbeat that moves the chest by well under a millimeter.

Where it matters

Clinical early-warning systems, driver stress and fatigue detection, and sleep monitoring. Cardiovascular disease caused 19.8 M deaths in 2022 (WHO).

Signal pipeline

From radar echoes to a heartbeat

Capture FMCW chirps

The TI AWR1642BOOST transmits frequency-modulated chirps sweeping 77 → 81 GHz. The echo off the chest comes back slightly delayed, and its phase encodes the chest's tiny back-and-forth motion.

Decode I/Q into frames

Raw binary radar data is loaded, the interleaved in-phase / quadrature samples are converted to complex numbers, and everything is reshaped into frames × chirps × ADC samples × virtual antennas.

03 / 06

Find the chest's range bin

A range FFT turns each chirp into a distance profile. We pick the bin containing the chest and tune it for every scenario, because even a small misalignment picks up background clutter instead of breathing.

40–160 cmtuned per scenario
04 / 06

Fuse the antennas

Each virtual antenna sees its own multipath and angle-dependent noise. Averaging the phase across all eight antennas, frame by frame, cancels much of that noise and keeps the shared chest motion.

8 virtual antennasphase averaging
05 / 06

Unwrap and detrend

Phase wraps around at ±π, so it is unwrapped to remove the jumps, then detrended to remove slow drift. What remains is the chest displacement signal itself.

phase unwrapdetrend
06 / 06

Split into breath and heart

Band-pass filters matched to adult physiology separate the slow breathing rhythm from the much smaller, faster heartbeat. These two signals feed each of the three methods.

Three frameworks

What we benchmarked

Each method takes the preprocessed signal and estimates HR and BR its own way: classical spectral analysis, wavelet-based signal processing, and a learned model.

METHOD 1

Classical baseline · BPF + FFT

HR 9.95BR 12.9

Two complementary estimators: a periodogram picks the dominant frequency in each band, and time-domain peak counting counts cycles over the recording.

METHOD 2

DWT-Kalman-AR

HR 7.96BR 6.24

A Daubechies-4 wavelet splits the chest signal into five frequency bands. Heart rate is smoothed with a Kalman filter and read off an autoregressive spectrum. Breathing, which is slower and more regular, is counted directly from peaks.

METHOD 3

Deep learning · CNN + BiLSTM + Attention

HR 3.12BR 2.99

Two dedicated networks. The HR model reads 10-second amplitude + phase windows (1 s step) through residual 1-D convolutions and two BiLSTM layers. The BR model adds Bahdanau attention to focus on the cleanest breathing cycles over the full recording.

Huber lossAdam (HR) · AdamW (BR)cosine-decay LRearly stoppingaugment: scale · noise · shift

Deep learning wins on every metric

Too smooth. The baseline follows the general trend during stable periods, but its strong smoothing compresses the dynamic range. It consistently underestimates peak heart and breathing rates and can't keep up with rapid physiological changes.

Ablation: adding a denoising autoencoder hurt

We tested two denoising strategies for the deep model: band-pass filtering only, and band-pass filtering plus a denoising autoencoder (DAE). The model did better without the DAE. The network already learns to suppress the noise, so the extra stage was redundant and slightly counter-productive. Even with the DAE, the deep model still outperformed both signal-processing methods.

Data & setup

Diverse physiological scenarios

We use the public mm-Wave FMCW Radar Vital Sign Monitoring Dataset (Sadeghi et al., 2024). It covers resting, elevated heart rate, asthma and meditation, with a Polar H10 chest belt as heart-rate ground truth and counted breaths as breathing-rate ground truth.

Hardware

TI AWR1642 EVM, DCA1000 capture board, and both mounted in the radar holder.

Discussion

Limitations & next steps

Limitations

  • Class imbalance. Only one meditation participant and two asthma participants, so the model learns a bias toward the majority conditions.
  • Sparse BR labels. One breathing-rate ground-truth value per recording, compared with one HR value per second, limits BR training.
  • No pretraining. No standardized mmWave vital-sign dataset with a matching radar configuration exists, and mismatched configs risk treating real signal as noise.
  • Small dataset. To limit overfitting we controlled model size and used dropout, early stopping, augmentation and learning-rate scheduling.

Future work

  • Expand the dataset with more subjects and more physiological conditions.
  • Explore and stack other time-series deep models with built-in denoising.
  • Monitor multiple subjects simultaneously.
  • Deploy in clinical settings for continuous, contactless early-warning monitoring.
